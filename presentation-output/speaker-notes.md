## Editorial and source decisions

- Only the attached skeleton was read; none of its companion files or other repository files were consulted.
- The US chart is an index derived from the primary publication’s rounded 50% callback advantage. It is not an absolute callback-rate chart. The India and parenthood findings remain qualitative.
- The caste-thread URL was absent; its panel is explicitly illustrative, with no fabricated quotation or screenshot.
- The children’s-book text/image gap is attributed specifically to female characters.
- Test performance is separated from judgment in the synthesis.
- References cover sources used in this deck, rather than claiming to reproduce the unseen essay bibliography.
- No external photos, memes or screenshots are used; all visuals are original diagrams and editable shapes.

## 01. Boys don’t cry, girls can’t code

**Target: 0:00–0:30**

- Ask the room to finish: “Boys don’t ___.” “Girls can’t ___.” Pause.

- Everyone recognises these phrases. Recognition does not imply agreement, and agreement does not necessarily determine action.

- The title names stereotypes for examination; it does not endorse them. Introduce the talk as a thematic literature review.

## 02. From a checklist to a language model

**Target: 0:30–1:30**

- Katz and Braly asked Princeton students to assign traits to groups. Caliskan and colleagues measured associations in word embeddings trained on web text.

- These are not equivalent measurements, nor a longitudinal demonstration that the same belief survived unchanged.

- Stereotypes predate social media. Ask what digital circulation changes: who can speak, how far a label travels, and how it can be reused.

Sources: Katz & Braly (1933); Caliskan, Bryson & Narayanan (2017)

## 03. How do stereotypes persist—and change?

**Target: 1:30–2:15**

- Describe the scope as thematic, not exhaustive: race, caste, gender, class, age, disability, religion and sexuality. Several studies also concern the UK and other settings.

- Do not claim that the same categories or mechanisms operate identically everywhere.

- The route moves from learning to decisions, representation, online circulation and unresolved questions.

## 04. Knowing is not the same as endorsing

**Target: 2:15–3:45**

- Define the three concepts separately. Stereotypes may appear positive; prejudice concerns evaluation; discrimination concerns treatment.

- Devine distinguishes automatic activation from controlled reflection. Knowing a cultural stereotype does not establish that someone endorses it.

- Fiske’s model uses perceived warmth and competence. The grid is schematic, not a ranking of actual groups. “Warm but incapable” may sound affectionate but can justify exclusion.

Sources: Devine (1989); Fiske, Cuddy, Glick & Xu (2002)

## 05. What did the study actually measure?

**Target: 3:45–4:45**

- This is the central method question for the whole presentation. Pick two contrasts rather than reading every cell.

- A hiring audit observes employer response; it does not reveal the employer’s thoughts. A media content study observes representation; it does not measure what readers took away.

- Methods are complementary. The issue is whether the conclusion exceeds the measurement.

## 06. The lesson starts early

**Target: 4:45–6:15**

- Bian and colleagues studied children’s judgments and interest in activities. Avoid presenting the slide text as a verbatim quotation from the paper.

- At age six, girls were less likely than boys to associate their own gender with brilliance, and showed lower interest in activities framed around brilliance.

- This does not by itself explain later careers.

- Ridgeway and Correll offer a theoretical bridge: repeated interactions can turn shared gender beliefs into durable differences in authority and opportunity. Distinguish that theory from this experiment.

Sources: Bian, Leslie & Cimpian (2017); Ridgeway & Correll (2004)

## 07. A link is not a causal arrow

**Target: 6:15–7:00**

- Checkpoint: aim to be at seven minutes by the end of this slide.

- Ward and Grower review evidence including meta-analyses that find a small, consistent association between television viewing and stereotyped gender beliefs.

- That association alone does not establish that viewing caused the beliefs. Family and peers may shape both; children may select content consistent with existing beliefs.

- Power matters: one student can use a label to judge while another has to defend against it.

Sources: Ward & Grower (2020)

## 08. A decision before a meeting

**Target: 7:00–9:00**

- Allow two minutes. In the US study, fictitious applications to Boston and Chicago job advertisements were randomly assigned names signalling race. The published abstract reports 50% more callbacks for White-sounding names.

- The chart is a relative index derived from that rounded finding, not absolute callback percentages. It starts at zero; Black-sounding names are set to 100.

- Thorat and Attewell use matched applications to Indian private-sector jobs; lower response for Dalit and Muslim applicants is shown qualitatively. Do not compare chart magnitudes across the studies.

- Correll and colleagues combine a laboratory study and an employer audit. Keep evaluations and proposed salaries in the lab distinct from actual employer callbacks.

- Race and caste have different histories. Audits identify differential responses to signals in the studied setting, not employers’ private motives, final hiring or later pay.

Sources: Bertrand & Mullainathan (2004); Thorat & Attewell (2007); Correll, Benard & Paik (2007)

## 09. Who controls the test conditions?

**Target: 9:00–10:30**

- If behind schedule, give only the US example and the boundary of the claim, then move on.

- Steele and Aronson manipulated the framing of a difficult test. Hoff and Pandey varied the salience of caste in a task setting. Both concern performance under particular conditions; they do not explain all educational inequality.

- Mention resources, teaching and exclusion as additional factors. Stereotype-threat findings have generated debate about generalisability and replication; do not frame one experiment as a universal effect.

- Shildrick and MacDonald use interviews in north-east England: people may distance themselves from poverty while reproducing stigma toward others. The on-screen line is a paraphrase, not a participant quote.

- Schools and researchers control task framing; students have less say. Checkpoint: around 10:30.

Sources: Steele & Aronson (1995); Hoff & Pandey (2006); Shildrick & MacDonald (2013)

## 10. Visible in the picture. Missing from the story.

**Target: 10:30–12:30**

- Adukia and colleagues analyse children’s books across the last century. The text–image gap on this slide refers specifically to female characters. They also document underrepresentation of Black and Latinx people.

- Fonseca and colleagues analyse caste coverage in three Indian newspapers in 2016–2017. Reporting violence matters; the question is what becomes invisible when other dimensions of life receive less attention.

- Purdie-Vaughns and Eibach provide a model of intersectional invisibility: people with multiple subordinated identities may not fit the prototype of any one category. Ask “which women?”

- These findings describe content and representation. They do not measure audience beliefs.

Sources: Adukia et al. (2023); Fonseca et al. (2019); Purdie-Vaughns & Eibach (2008)

## 11. Three ways a stereotype travels

**Target: 12:30–14:30**

- Springfield: the anti-Haitian pet-eating allegation was false. Authorities reported no credible evidence. PolitiFact documents its political and meme circulation. Do not reproduce the allegation as a plausible claim.

- Girl math: a gendered label can be self-directed humour or interpreted as sexist. My analysis: cost-per-use reasoning is not inherently gendered; the label makes gender relevant. The Guardian article documents disagreement, not a population attitude.

- Ilbury studies “Hun” memes, not girl math. Use the paper as an analytical comparison about recurring personae, not direct evidence for this trend.

- The attached skeleton did not include a URL for its caste-meme thread. Panel three is explicitly an illustrative discussion pattern; it is not a quotation, screenshot or verified 2024 case. Do not present it as collected data.

- Speakers, reach and consequences differ across these examples.

Sources: PolitiFact (2024); Demopoulos (2023); Ilbury (2022). Third panel is illustrative, not sampled evidence.

## 12. From being described to describing yourself

**Target: 14:30–15:30**

- Thoreau studies a sample of writing on the BBC’s Ouch website. Disabled authors portray multidimensional lives, with humour and irony. That does not make one site representative of all disability experiences.

- Attri and colleagues examine Dalit users’ construction of histories online and the unequal conditions surrounding it. Counterpublics are spaces where people excluded from dominant discussion can speak and build shared accounts.

- Self-representation shifts who can describe a group. Whether it changes wider audiences or institutions requires further evidence. Harassment and unequal attention can persist.

Sources: Thoreau (2006); Attri, Ramya, Glozer & Soundararajan (2026)

## 13. Do not jump across the evidence gap

**Target: 15:30–17:00**

- The staircase is a map of measurement targets, not an inevitable causal chain or a ranking that makes interviews less valuable.

- A shared description is not an employer decision. A news story is not a reader’s belief. A biased embedding is not proof of discrimination by every deployed system.

- Steele and Aronson measure test performance, so they are deliberately not placed on the judgment step.

- Stereotypes may involve mixed warmth and competence, not simply hostility. The motherhood study shows that categories interact.

- Paluck and colleagues review prejudice-reduction experiments and highlight limits in evidence for lasting, consequential change. Related questions about power do not erase different social histories.

Sources: Katz & Braly (1933); Fiske et al. (2002); Correll et al. (2007); Paluck et al. (2021)

## 14. Four questions worth following

**Target: 17:00–18:30**

- Each proposed question follows from a limit already named. Content evidence lacks reception; posts cannot establish belief change; visibility may not change treatment; short-term effects may fade.

- These are proposed directions, not a claim that no studies on these topics exist.

- Pettigrew and Tropp find an overall relationship between intergroup contact and lower prejudice. An antagonistic comment thread is not equivalent to every form of contact.

- Consent matters in longitudinal work: a public post is not blanket agreement to participate in research.

Sources: Pettigrew & Tropp (2006); Paluck et al. (2021); Purdie-Vaughns & Eibach (2008)

## 16. A record changes the encounter

**Target: Viva only**

- Pager studied entry-level hiring in Milwaukee with matched male testers. Explain the manipulation of record status and the comparison across race. Avoid generalising to all labour markets.

- Source: https://doi.org/10.1086/374403

## 17. Caste descriptions and social distance

**Target: Viva only**

- The study used Patna University students. Keep expressed association preference separate from observed conduct. Historical comparisons are not repeated observation of the same people.

- Source: https://doi.org/10.1093/sf/46.1.42

Sources: Sinha & Sinha (1967)

## 18. When a stereotype becomes self-relevant

**Target: Viva only**

- Distinguish the review/theory from the experimental and longitudinal studies it draws upon. It proposes routes through which stereotypes can become self-relevant with ageing.

- Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01662.x

Sources: Levy (2009)

## 19. A pandemic-era association

**Target: Viva only**

- Separate social-media use from trust in its information. Existing attitudes and other variables may explain part of the association. Do not infer that all users or all platforms act identically.

- Source: https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/8d823430-d5d6-41fc-9464-a2f4828c271a
